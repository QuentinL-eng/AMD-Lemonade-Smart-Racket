Technical white paper

Tennis Buddee: a smart racket with a local, tool-calling coach

Sensing, on-racket computation, physics-based what-if analysis and a small language model served by AMD Lemonade

Abstract

Recreational tennis players receive no usable feedback about their own strokes. Tennis Buddee puts a sensor insert in the handle of an ordinary racket: a six-axis inertial unit with two accelerometer ranges, a microphone, and a Bluetooth LE microcontroller. The racket detects each hit, computes an 80-byte shot summary on board, and sends it to an iPhone, Mac or Apple Watch. The apps turn the summary into swing speed, face and path angles, stroke type, contact point, ball speed and spin, and run a bounded physics sensitivity model to estimate what a swing change would do. A tool-calling language model chooses which tools to call and explains the result, while application code supplies every number.

This paper documents the hardware, firmware, signal processing, models and evaluation. On a 243-case validation sample, a QLoRA-tuned Qwen2.5-1.5B reached 212 strict passes (87.2%) on our own Transformers server, within 0.5 points of a historical hosted baseline of 87.7%. The final round, merged to GGUF and served by Lemonade itself, passed 197 of 243 validation cases (81.1%, 95% interval 75.7 to 85.5) and 136 of 166 on a test split run once (81.9%, 75.4 to 87.0), with no response errors and 73 more validation passes than the untuned Qwen3-1.7B on Lemonade (124, 51.0%). Getting there depended on a finding about grammar-constrained tool calling: Lemonade delivers tool schemas with sorted keys, and llama.cpp forces call arguments into that order, so earlier Lemonade rounds scored 46 to 49% until training wrote arguments in the allowed order. Field recordings on court drove the racket architecture: impact detection on the racket, 80-byte summaries computed on board, and an acknowledged window path with a flash backlog.

Keywords wearable sensing, impact detection, edge inference, tool-calling LLM, QLoRA, llama.cpp grammar, grounded generation, Lemonade

1Introduction

A smart racket produces numbers that a recreational player cannot interpret: contact position, swing speed, face angle. The question a player asks is "what would happen if I changed my swing?" Answering it honestly needs two things that usually pull apart. One is a simulator whose outputs are trustworthy and bounded. The other is a language model small enough to run on hardware the player owns, that can choose the right tool and say the result in plain words.

The design rule that holds the project together is that the model never invents a number. It selects tools. The application computes and returns every figure, and the evaluation scorer rejects any number in an answer that is not found in a tool result.

This document makes five contributions.

  1. A complete description of the sensing hardware and firmware: wiring, sensor ranges, impact detection, on-racket shot analysis, a Bluetooth protocol with acknowledgements and a flash backlog, and the on-racket acoustic model (sections 3 to 6).
  2. The physics and rules that convert a hit window into shot metrics, and the sensitivity model used for what-if questions (sections 5 and 7).
  3. The agent: ten tools, a bounded loop, and code-level number grounding (section 8).
  4. A reproducible tuning and evaluation record across seven scored rounds and two serving paths (sections 9 and 10).
  5. A finding for anyone fine-tuning a tool-calling model for Lemonade: the property order of a tool schema is part of the model's output contract, and training on the order the server allows recovers the score (sections 10.4 and 10.5).
How to read the evidence

Statements are tagged where it matters. measured means a file in the repository holds the result. design means the code does this but no outcome was measured. planned means it has not been done. Section 14 states the scope of the evidence.

3Hardware

3.1Components and wiring

The insert is built around a Seeed XIAO ESP32-C3, a single-core RISC-V microcontroller with Bluetooth 5 and no floating-point unit. The inertial sensor is an ST LSM6DSV320X on the STEVAL-MKI251A adapter board. It contains a gyroscope, a ±16 g accelerometer and a ±320 g high-g accelerometer in one package. An INMP441 digital MEMS microphone gives audio of the impact. A single-cell LiPo with a power button supplies the board. An earlier build used an MPU6050 and an H3LIS331DL as two separate chips; the single package replaced both, and the firmware converts its output into the older units so the apps and recordings stay compatible.

3.2Mechanical placement

The insert sits in the butt end of the handle. The CAD plan from the first build put the board at 0 to 30 mm from the butt flange, the inertial sensors at 18 to 39 mm and the microphone near 55 mm, facing into the handle, with the battery beyond the insert wrapped in foam so it cannot rattle. The racket profile defaults are 325 g, 686 mm long, balance near 320 mm and a string-bed centre 520 mm from the butt. The motion analysis uses an effective radius of 1.15 m, a single calibration scalar that folds the arm into the lever, not a physical length. Figure 4 shows these positions and the contact-point axes the software reports.

3.3Why two accelerometers and a microphone

A ball impact is a short, large jolt, while a swing is slow and rotational. No single range covers both. The low-g channel reads gravity and gentle motion, so it gives orientation. The high-g channel survives the impact itself: on the club recordings the median event peaked at 21 g, the 90th percentile at 85 g and the maximum at 233 g. The gyroscope gives swing speed and the rotation changes that locate the contact. The microphone adds a second, independent view of the same impact, and its first 32 ms carries information about where on the strings the ball landed. The firmware therefore has two ways to declare a hit (section 4.2) and one model that reads the sound.

4Firmware

The firmware runs a sampler, a microphone task, a shot task and the BLE loop on the ESP32-C3. Figure 5 shows the data path. Two design choices shaped it. The first is that the racket detects hits itself and sends only the samples around them, because the radio could not carry the raw stream. The second is that the racket computes the shot, so the phone can show it from 80 bytes without waiting for 9 kB of window data.

4.1Impact detection

A hit is declared by either of two rules, both over the high-g channel. The motion rule needs a large jump during a fast swing. The microphone rule handles blocks, soft volleys and drop shots, which are too gentle or too still for the first rule: the microphone must have triggered, a smaller high-g jump must confirm the racket was struck, and there must have been some racket movement in the previous 300 ms, so a tap on a still racket is not counted.

4.2Orientation

The racket runs a Mahony complementary filter [1] on a 512-slot ring buffer, with proportional gain 2.0 and integral gain 0.05. The accelerometer corrects the gyro integration only when the sensed acceleration is within 0.85 to 1.15 g and the rotation is below 3 rad/s, that is, when the racket is nearly still and gravity dominates. The orientation at the window start is sent with every chunk so the app can continue integrating without a gap. The same filter exists in Swift and in the shot analyzer, so a window analysed on the racket and on the phone follow the same arithmetic.

4.3Shot analysis on board

The racket's shot.cpp is a step-by-step port of the Swift ShotAnalyzer, using a calibration and racket profile that the app sends once (command SHOT_CONFIG, 36 floats plus a handedness flag). It returns one 80-byte TBShotSummary. Because the C3 has no floating-point unit, this is emulated in software; the firmware logs the worst-case microseconds per summary in its status line. A parity test builds the C++ and runs it against the Swift on the same windows to hold the two within tight tolerances.

4.4Protocol, acknowledgements and backlog

Firmware 1.6 added a reliable window path. The protocol header carries a firmware minor version of 9 at the time of writing. Each hit produces three messages in order: a hit notice, a summary, and 56 window chunks of 8 samples each. The app acknowledges a window by boot number and sequence, and asks for specific chunks again if some are missing. A window not acknowledged within 5 seconds is sent again. Figure 6 shows the exchange and the exact byte layouts. The summary is about 1% of the window by size (80 against 9,296 bytes), which is why the app can show a shot almost at once.

Six windows are held in RAM. When all six are taken, or when no phone is connected and the racket has been still for 3 seconds, windows are written to a spare 1.375 MB flash partition in 12 KB records, up to 117, and sent oldest first once the link is free, across reboots. Erasing flash stalls the CPU for tens of milliseconds, so erases only happen while the racket is idle, and writes go one 256-byte page per loop pass. Once an app has requested this mode, the racket keeps capturing even with no phone connected, and that flag survives reboots in non-volatile storage.

4.5Tap gestures

A knock on the handle is a sharp jolt on the low-g channel with a short ring-down and almost no gyro or high-g signature, which separates it from a ball impact. The detector tracks a running baseline of the acceleration vector, triggers when the vector moves 300 mg from it, keeps a 256-sample window, and reports one gesture (single, double, triple) once taps stop. It also outputs a push direction. A zone classifier reports where along the handle a tap landed; its ten-feature nearest-centroid model is fitted from taps at known positions with the capture and fit tools in the repository, and until then the zone field reads "unknown".

4.6Verification of the firmware

  • The backlog runs on a laptop against a fake flash that, like the real one, can only clear bits between erases, under address and undefined-behaviour sanitizers.
  • The C++ shot analyzer is checked against Swift by ShotParityTests, which builds the C++ itself.
  • The C++ acoustic model matches Python on all 296 clips (274 reviewed and 22 synthetic), with a maximum feature error of 5×10−7 and identical decisions measured.

5Shot metrics

From one window the analyzer computes the quantities in Figure 7. Everything is geometry on the gyro, the orientation and the high-g channel; nothing is learned, and the constants are set by hand or from small sessions.

5.1Speed, angles and spin

Rotation about the shaft only spins the head in place, so only the components across it move the sweet spot. With ω the mean gyro vector over the 8 ms before contact and r the effective radius:

vR = r · √(ωy² + ωz²)(1)
e = e0 · max(0.55, 1 − 0.035 d), vball = (1 + e) vR + e u(2)
rpm = k · vbrush / rball · 60 / 2π, vbrush = vR · |v̂ − (v̂·n̂) n̂|(3)

Here d is the distance of the contact from the sweet spot in centimetres, e0 = 0.42 is the apparent coefficient of restitution at the sweet spot [9], u is the incoming ball speed, which the racket cannot measure and sets to zero, k = 0.7 is the fraction of brushing speed that becomes ball surface speed, rball = 33.5 mm, and n̂ and v̂ are the face normal and the sweet-spot velocity direction in the world frame. With r = 1.15 m, equation (1) and (2) predict ball speed of 1.633 × |ωyz| for a centred hit on a still ball. Face and path angles are the arcsine of the dot product of the face normal and of the velocity with world up. Spin is labelled flat below 600 rpm, topspin when the brushing component points up, and slice otherwise.

5.2Contact point

Two measurements locate the ball on the strings. A hit off to one side of the sweet spot twists the racket, so the change in rotation about the shaft relative to the change across it, the roll ratio, gives the lateral offset: x = −1.65 × roll ratio, clamped to ±12 cm. Along the strings, a hit toward the tip excites the frame's ring-down more strongly. The analyzer takes the high-g face-normal signal from 4 to 60 ms after contact, forms the difference of a two-sample and a four-sample moving average (a band-pass near 120 to 250 Hz), and normalises its RMS by the impulse. That gives |y| = min(14, 10 × ring). The sign comes from the jolt, the velocity change of the handle along the face normal over 6 ms: a positive jolt means the handle was pushed back with the ball, so the contact was toward the throat. Below a jolt of 0.02 the sign is unknown and |y| is scaled by 0.3.

6Sweet-spot model

The one model that runs on the racket predicts how far from the sweet spot the ball hit, from the first 32 ms of the impact sound. It is a ridge regression, chosen because it is small, deterministic and can be evaluated with integer-friendly arithmetic. Figure 8 shows the features and the decision.

6.1Results

On 274 reviewed clips from two sessions, with 5-fold cross-validation that holds out whole 8-trigger blocks, the ridge model reaches 67.2% accuracy on the sweet/off-centre call against 60.6% for always answering off-centre, and a mean radial error of 1.83 cm against 2.41 cm for predicting the training median. A group bootstrap over the 37 blocks gives an accuracy interval of 59.6 to 74.8%. Five other approaches were compared (nested selection, a multi-task network, scattering features with classical models and others) and none beat the ridge by more than noise, so the small, deterministic model is the one deployed on the racket. Parity tests confirm that the C++ matches Python on every clip.

The model's strength is the distance estimate: outside the 3 to 5 cm band its call is right 73% of the time. The recorded next steps are to record a third session on another day with the microphone, to show 3 to 5 cm as "close" instead of forcing a yes/no call, to confirm the label scale between sessions, and to refit on all 274 clips.

7What-if simulator

The ShotSimulator is a bounded sensitivity model, not a ball-flight predictor. It takes six parameters (swing speed, path angle, face angle, contact x and y, incoming speed), clamps them to physical ranges, and applies equations (2) and (3) with the face-minus-path angle setting the brushing component:

vbrush = vR · sin(path − face), vball = e u + (1 + e) vR(4)

The tool applies a change to the player's mean motion-enabled shot of a chosen stroke. Mic-only and gyro-saturated shots are excluded because they carry no swing. Inputs are clamped (swing ±30%, angles ±15°, contact ±5 cm), and incoming speed is assumed: 43.2 km/h for groundstrokes, 54 for volleys and 0 for serves. The tool returns a verdict on each effect rather than leaving the model to judge size:

  • Spin gain is decisive if the new spin is at least max(1.3 × before, before + 400 rpm, 1200 rpm).
  • Pace gain is decisive if the new ball speed is at least 1.05 × before.
  • Contact is centred if its distance from the sweet spot is at most 2.0 cm.

These bars are shared by the Swift code, the Python mirror and the evaluator's goal rule. The model is a sensitivity model: it ranks and sizes changes on the player's own average shot rather than predicting absolute ball speed, and the dataset teaches the agent to call every result an estimate. A wall drill (self-fed hits at 4 m, ball speed from the timing of racket pop and wall thud) is specified to fit the effective radius and restitution against measured ball speed planned.

8Racket agent

8.1Loop and tools

The agent sends the player's question and ten tool schemas to an OpenAI-compatible chat endpoint and executes any tool calls on the device. Figure 10 shows the loop. The first user message is exactly what the app sends, and the model's first move is to call get_session_digest. The loop is bounded to five rounds with at most eight calls per round, and the last round sends no tools and asks for the final answer. If the reply is empty, calls tools on the last round, or fans out beyond eight calls, the app raises an error and falls back to a built-in engine.

The tools split into four groups: session data (get_session_digest, query_shots, list_shots, compare_sessions), live racket state (get_live_state, read_live_sensors, get_racket_status), the simulator (simulate_change), and notes (recall_notes, remember_note). Validation rejects unknown tools and keys, missing required arguments, strings outside an enum, and non-finite numbers. Notes are capped at 240 characters, two per answer and a fixed total per player, and the tool description tells the model to treat their text as information, never as instructions.

8.2Keeping numbers honest

The system prompt requires that only numbers returned by tools be cited, that every sentence stating a simulation number contain the word "estimated", that net clearance, landing depth and trajectory are never claimed, and that the model quote numbers as given and never do arithmetic on them. A prompt cannot guarantee any of this, so two code-level checks back it up. The evaluator rejects any number in an answer that is not in a tool result, within a small tolerance. In the browser path, the model finishes with a present_answer call whose parts are either short prose or a reference (tool-call id and a JSON pointer) into a tool result; a dependency-free renderer resolves each reference to the original finite number and rejects any digit or common number word in prose. This prevents invented numeric strings and complements the strict score. The browser path uses it today, and the native app is the next adopter design.

8.3Swing film

A second use of the same loop produces a short animation of the player's swing and the recommended change. The model may emit only a small script (stroke, up to two levers within fixed ranges that equal a simulation it ran, and short titles with no digits). A validator rejects anything else and returns the reasons to the model at most twice. A fixed renderer draws the result, so the model never produces pixels and cannot invent a body, a court or a ball flight.

9Data and tuning

9.1Synthetic what-if conversations

No public dataset exists of tool-use conversations about racket data, so we generate them (Figure 11). A Python mirror of the Swift tools produces exactly the tool results the app would return, checked against Swift output in tests. Priors come from real data: racket speed at contact from 45 camera-confirmed hits (median 120 km/h, coefficient of variation 0.14), the shape of the contact cloud from 274 reviewed contacts, and link conditions from the club session. A club-like link makes 21% of shots mic-only because a shot that arrives before the previous window has finished sending loses its motion data, against 7% on a phone-like link. Swing path, face angle, stroke mix and injected technique flaws are assumed, not measured.

9.2Fine-tuning recipe

The base model is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct [4] (1,562,179,072 parameters, Apache-2.0, a documented tool-call template). We apply QLoRA [2], [3] with a 4-bit NF4 base with double quantization, rank 16, alpha 32, dropout 0.05 on all seven attention and MLP projection matrices, learning rate 2×10−4, AdamW, fp16 with gradient checkpointing, batch size 1 with 8 accumulation steps, seed 7 and a 4,608-token limit. Loss is taken on assistant messages only, with selective vocabulary logits to reduce memory; a CPU comparison against the full masked loss agreed within 10−6. Every one of the 1,809 training conversations fits within the limit (longest 4,509 tokens) and none is truncated. The adapter weights are not in the repository, and each run records the SHA-256 of its adapter and training file. Round 8 used the round 4 filtered set with the cleaner-contact and more-spin conversations repeated once (2,185 conversations) and 600 steps.

10Lemonade evaluation

10.1Method

Every engine is scored by one script, tools/agent_eval.py, on the same stratified sample of 243 player-split validation conversations (seed 7), with real tool dispatch from the Python mirror and the app's own limits (five rounds, eight calls). A case passes only if all of these hold: the first tool call is get_session_digest; every number in the answer appears in a tool result or the question; any simulation is labelled as an estimate; no unknown tool was called; and, for goal cases, the final simulation reaches the goal under the same rule the dataset uses. A request error counts as a failure. Intervals below are 95% Wilson intervals [7] over the 243 cases; they capture sampling uncertainty on this sample only, not variation across training seeds, since each configuration was trained once.

That filter is rejection sampling with the evaluator's rule and must be disclosed with any resulting score. Rounds 2 to 4 were selected on these validation cases; the test split was run once, on round 8 (section 10.5).

10.3Serving the same weights through Lemonade

Lemonade Server 2026.45.3 on Windows served an untuned Qwen3-1.7B (Q4_0) with 124 strict passes (51.0%), the starting point that motivated tuning. We then merged the round 4 adapter into fp16 weights, converted to GGUF with llama.cpp's converter, quantised to Q8_0 (1.5 GB) and loaded it as a normal Lemonade model. A 243-case run takes about six minutes this way, against about 2.5 hours on the Transformers/NF4 server. The score was 118, which led to the finding in section 10.4.

10.4Finding: Lemonade sorts schema keys, and the grammar enforces that order

On goal cases the effect was narrow. The model's call to simulate_change was {"type":"forehand"} with no change, so the tool reported "no change" and the model concluded the contact was already centred. Across the four Lemonade runs trained on other argument orders, the last simulation in a goal case applied no change in 111, 119, 123 and 123 of 123 goal cases, against 0 of 123 on the Transformers server and 0 of 123 for round 8, which was trained on the allowed order (Figure 14). A paired comparison of round 4 on the two servers has 94 cases that pass only on the Transformers server and none that pass only on Lemonade (McNemar χ² = 92.0 with continuity correction [8]).

Two explanations were tested and ruled out, each costing a training run. First, the prompt text does differ: the same request is 3,009 tokens through Lemonade and 3,022 sent straight to the llama.cpp backend, and sorting the tool schemas by hand reproduces 3,009 exactly. Training rounds 6 and 7 used that sorted text (round 7's training prompt matched what llama.cpp renders, character for character) and did not fix the problem. Second, the weights and the conversion are not broken: given the rendered prompt, the fp16 merge in Transformers and the Q8_0 GGUF in llama.cpp's completion endpoint both produced the correct call, {"type":"forehand","swing_percent":10}, for both key orders. The grammar was shown directly by changing only the order of properties for simulate_change and sending the same request to the backend:

Table 8. Arguments the model produced under each schema property order (llama.cpp backend).
Property orderArguments produced
session, type, swing_percent, … (the app's order)type, swing_percent (correct)
session, swing_percent, …, type (type last)session, swing_percent (type lost)

The recommendations are practical. Test a tuned model through the server it will be deployed on, because a no-grammar serving path can overstate quality. Make the model's argument order the order the grammar allows: --sort-tool-keys in the training script renders sorted parameters and writes every training call's arguments alphabetically, with type last. Round 8 uses it and recovered the Lemonade path (section 10.5). Training on shuffled orders is a natural way to make a small model independent of argument order. A one-command check, tools/lemonade_schema_order_check.py, shows whether a server normalises key order by comparing prompt-token counts for the two orders (204 and 204 through Lemonade, 205 and 204 straight to llama.cpp). A draft upstream compatibility report is prepared.

10.5Round 8: trained for the order Lemonade allows

The test split scores within a point of validation, which suggests the validation selection did not inflate the result by much, although a single run has its own sampling interval.

10.6AMD hardware evidence

The full evaluations ran on a Windows laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 4070 through Lemonade's llama.cpp CUDA backend. On AMD hardware, the official Lemonade container loaded Qwen3-1.7B (Q4_0) on a 4 GiB RX 580 under Vulkan, using about 1.59 GB of VRAM, and answered a bounded 16-token request in about 0.27 s end to end through an SSH tunnel. Tests on that machine run under a watchdog that stops the container at 75 °C or low memory. A full evaluation on AMD hardware, and a larger tuned model on a machine with more unified memory, are the next steps.

11Field findings

These results come from the real racket and shaped the design more than any benchmark.

  • On-court streaming drove impact mode. Fifteen raw-stream exports from a club session (about 86 minutes) show about 17% of the 1 kHz stream delivered (13 to 30% per recording; median 214 samples/s), in runs of one or two packets about one connection interval apart. A 448-sample window then takes about 2.1 s to send. This evidence led to impact mode, on-board summaries and the backlog, which make delivery independent of link throughput.
  • The gyro range is now set by readback. Eight of twelve recordings flatten at 34.87 rad/s, the int16 limit at 16.4 LSB/dps. ST's driver changes full scale only with the gyro powered down; firmware 1.6 switches the range that way and checks the readback, and from 1.8 the racket is pinned to ±2000 dps.
  • High-g range. One sample reached 200.7 g, so the wire range was widened to ±400 g in firmware 1.6, at about 0.2 g per step against 0.35 g RMS sensor noise at rest.
  • Stroke typing. On a separate reviewed session of 29 forehands, the analyzer typed 28 correctly (interval 82.8 to 99.4%). Validation on backhands, serves and volleys over more sessions and players is the next step.
  • Replay finds the hits. On the club data the detector found 124 hits against 151 high-g events in the received samples, which shows the rule fires on court motion.

12Apps, emulator and privacy

One SwiftUI codebase serves iPhone and Mac, with a Watch companion for workouts and haptic feedback, and an optional glasses display. RacketKit is a Swift package holding decoding, the filter, the analyzer, calibration flows (hold still, tip up, flat, a drop grid with tap-to-label, a 3×3 sweet-spot check), recording and replay, tennis score keeping and a simulated racket, so the apps can be built and demonstrated with no hardware. Sessions live in SwiftData and sync through the player's private iCloud. A browser emulator replays labelled professional matches through the same data path, with racket tracks from SAM 3.1 and stroke types from the F3Tennis model, and always shows a provenance banner stating what is measured and what is illustrative; a local Node server routes its agent calls to Lemonade on loopback.

Shot history stays on the player's devices until the player shares a session total with a friend. The agent sends the question and the tool inputs needed for an answer to the server the player configured, which may be the same device. The friend-sharing backend is a Supabase Postgres schema with row-level security enabled and all direct table access revoked; every operation is a security-definer function that checks the caller. It stores only profiles, friendships, shared matches and points, and per-match totals (shots, sweet-spot percentage, average and top ball speed). For phone-to-laptop use, the setup guide has the laptop's Lemonade server listen on a trusted private network.

The repository holds roughly 144 Swift test functions and 175 Python test definitions by a simple count. The Python suites and the evaluator run on Windows, and the Swift package tests run with swift test on macOS.

13TennisRobot: a rig for repeatable ground truth

Ground truth for ball speed and spin is the main gap in the racket's validation, and a robot arm swinging the racket at a point on the strings would supply repeatable hits. The TennisRobot framework pairs a camera with an Epson C8 arm and an RC700-E controller. The Linux side detects the ball with an HSV colour threshold (not a learned detector), fits a six-state flight model with quadratic drag and optional Magnus lift by damped Gauss–Newton, finds where the path crosses a strike plane, and sends the controller three poses, a speed and a lead time. All real-time motion stays on the controller, and the Linux side never streams servo targets.

The pipeline and its 49 tests run in simulation: on a slow feed it hit 4 of 4 balls with a median total error of 6.0 cm, and across five seeds 18 of 19 strikes hit with no faults. Bring-up is staged, from camera only, to a read-only controller, to strike commands, and the Epson RC+ simulator project already holds the racket mounted on the arm with its payload set. Real-time motion and the emergency stop stay on the controller; the software checks are not safety-rated.

14Scope of the evidence

The results reflect what each experiment was designed to show.

  • Synthetic evaluation. The 243 cases use simulated players and a Python mirror of the tools, so they test tool choice, grounding and goal-reaching, not tennis improvement.
  • Validation and test. Rounds 2 to 4 were selected on the validation cases and the training data was filtered with the evaluator's goal rule; the test split was run once, on the final round 8 model (136 of 166, 81.9%). Each configuration was trained once.
  • Serving path. The 87.2% was measured on our Transformers server. The 81.1% and 81.9% were measured through Lemonade itself, on merged fp16 weights quantised to Q8_0, which are close to, not identical to, the NF4 model.
  • Comparisons. The hosted baseline is a single retained aggregate, and Qwen3-1.7B differs from the tuned model in base model, quantisation and runtime.
  • Sensing. The acoustic model is trained on two sessions, ball-speed constants await the wall drill, and stroke typing is validated on forehands from one reviewed session.
  • Players. A small player-trial protocol is prepared for usability feedback.
  • Platforms. Most development ran on Windows; the native apps and Swift tests run on a Mac.

15Future work

  1. Analyse the remaining round 8 misses (mainly more-spin goals), try shuffled argument orders, and separate the effect of argument order from the repeated data and longer training.
  2. Fit the ball-speed radius from the wall drill and the simulator's restitution from completed what-if trials, then regenerate the dataset.
  3. Record a third sweet-spot session on court with the microphone, audit label scales between sessions, and send distance or a "close" state on the wire.
  4. Fit the tap-zone model from taps at known positions.
  5. Run the native app against Lemonade on a device and move the browser's number renderer into Swift.
  6. Compare a tuned 7B-class model with the 1.5B model on a machine with more unified memory, using the same scorer.
  7. Send the schema-order compatibility report upstream after rerunning it on the latest Lemonade release.
